# Learning design for Soccer IQ (age 5–6)

Research notes on **how to teach** the skill tree, not what is in it. Each section ends with a recommendation.

**Evidence warning:** almost no research studies 5-year-olds learning sport decisions from a screen. Most findings below come from adjacent areas: game-based learning in older kids, preschool memory and media research, and video training in teen or adult athletes. Where the evidence is thin, this doc says so.

---

## Summary of recommendations

| Question | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| 1. Match vs. drills | **Hybrid.** Short drills teach one skill. A short "match" mode mixes learned skills. Session about 5–10 minutes. |
| 2. Feedback | **Deterministic** at first. Let him make mistakes, then show a short replay of the better choice with a highlight and one spoken cue. Praise the choice, not the kid. |
| 3. Intro clips | **In-game animated demo**, 10–20 seconds, same view as the game. Real clips are optional extras in parent mode. |
| 4. Review | **Leitner boxes counted in sessions, not days.** Mix 1–2 review rounds into each session. Mastery = 3 first-try correct across 2+ sessions. |
| 5. Pre-reader UI | **Large tap targets, recorded audio (ideally a parent's voice), stickers and kit unlocks tied to playing, not to perfect scores.** |
| 6. Transfer | **Weak evidence alone; much better with parent cue words on the field.** Use the same 1–2 word cue in game and on the field. |
| 7. Existing apps | None target age 5. Borrow the "pause and choose" pattern from adult trainers; avoid their text and complexity. |

## 1. Whole match vs. isolated scenarios

**Recommendation: hybrid. Teach each skill in short drills, then unlock a short "mini-match" that mixes skills he already knows.**

What the research says:

- **Games need instructional support to teach.** A meta-analysis of 107 studies found that support inside games (feedback, modeling, reflection) improves learning (d ≈ 0.34). Pure free play teaches less. ([Wouters & van Oostendorp, 2013](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13608021/05.pdf))
- **Games beat normal teaching only modestly, and multiple sessions help.** Serious games were more effective than conventional instruction (d ≈ 0.29), and more so with several sessions and with other teaching added. ([Wouters et al., 2013, summary](https://karlkapp.com/?p=4333))
- **Distracting extras hurt learning.** "Seductive details" (decorative animation, sounds, side content) tend to lower learning, and children may be more affected because of smaller working memory. ([Seductive details overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seductive_details)) A full match has a lot of action that is not the lesson.
- **Real youth coaching already uses a hybrid.** U.S. Soccer's Play-Practice-Play model is: play a small game, practice one focused activity, then play again. ([Iowa Soccer guide](https://www.iowasoccer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/169/2023/09/coach_play_practice_play.pdf), [U.S. Soccer U6 guide](https://www.ussoccer.com/us-way/player-development/coaching-guide/u6))

Why hybrid fits a 5-year-old:

- **Drills** give many decisions per minute and a clear "this is the lesson" moment. That matches deliberate practice: one goal, immediate feedback, many repeats.
- **A mini-match** gives the FIFA feel and the reward of "a real game." It also forces him to recognize *which* skill applies, which drills alone do not teach (see interleaving in section 4).
- **Keep the match short.** Make it a "first to 2 goals" 3v3 that pauses only for decisions on skills he has learned. A full simulated match with long stretches of no decisions will lose him.

**Session length:** about **5–10 minutes** of active play, ending on a win.

- There is no firm research number for 5-year-olds and app sessions. Studies with preschoolers used sessions of about 20–25 minutes with adult structure, twice a week. ([attention app study, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30853576/))
- The AAP advises limiting screen use for ages 2–5 to about 1 hour a day of high-quality content, ideally with a parent. ([HealthyChildren.org / AAP](https://www.healthychildren.org/English/family-life/Media/Pages/Where-We-Stand-TV-Viewing-Time.aspx))
- An observational study linked more than 30 minutes a day of app use with lower inhibition scores later. It shows association, not cause. ([Academic Pediatrics, 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30853576/))
- Spacing (section 4) means several short sessions beat one long one.

A concrete session: 1 intro demo (if a new skill) → 6–8 drill rounds → 1 mini-match (once unlocked) → sticker. Stop there, even if he wants more. "Come back tomorrow" is a feature.

## 2. Feedback design

**Recommendation: deterministic outcomes, let him make real mistakes, then auto-play a short replay of the better choice with one spoken cue. Praise the choice and the looking, not "you're smart."**

### Deterministic vs. probabilistic

**Use deterministic outcomes for new skills.** The wrong choice always fails, and the right choice almost always works.

- Young children learn categories best from a reliable signal. 4-year-olds struggle more than 7-year-olds to pick out the feature that always predicts the answer. ([Deng & Sloutsky, 2016, via CogSci 2020](https://cognitivesciencesociety.org/cogsci20/papers/0249/index.html)) Noisy feedback makes this harder.
- Children "probability match" in noisy tasks and are slower than adults to change their choices. ([Plate et al., 2017, summary](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/probability-learning-changes-in-behavior-across-time-and-development/811079051161632768-6256)) If a bad pass sometimes works, a 5-year-old may keep trying it.
- Real soccer *is* probabilistic. Add a little noise later, only in mini-match mode and only for mastered skills. Even then, frame it: "Good choice! The keeper made a great save." That way the *choice* is always judged right or wrong, even when the *outcome* varies.

### Errorless vs. learning from errors

**Allow errors, but make the first tries easy and make errors cheap.**

- The evidence is mixed. Errorless methods cut mistakes during training, but the advantage often disappears at later testing and transfer. ([Frontiers 2026 meta-analysis on errorless motor learning](https://public-pages-files-2025.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1722743/pdf); [errorless fading vs. trial-and-error](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/learning-transfer-and-retention-of-errorless-fading-versus-trial-and-error-teaching/811628923732361217-9735)) In typical children, errorless learning showed no clear benefit.
- Surprise drives learning. Babies learn more about objects that break their predictions. ([Stahl & Feigenson, 2015](https://hub.jhu.edu/2015/04/02/surprise-babies-learning)) Making a prediction before seeing the result helps children learn, if they can use the surprise to update their belief. ([Brod et al., 2020](https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2021/23315/pdf/Developmental_Science_2020_3_Brod_et_al_Being_proven_wrong_A.pdf)) Tapping a choice *is* a prediction, so the game already has this built in.
- Too many errors cause frustration and quitting. A practical target is **about 70–85% correct**. If he misses twice in a row on one skill, make the next round easier (fewer defenders, more obvious open teammate).

Scaffolding ladder for each skill (an errorless-style ramp that still allows mistakes):

1. **Show:** demo plays (section 3).
2. **Hint:** the right option glows softly on the first 1–2 rounds.
3. **Choose:** no hint. Real mistakes are possible.
4. **Mixed:** the skill appears in review and in matches with no warning.

### Showing "why" to a pre-reader

- **Explanatory feedback beats right/wrong alone for transfer.** In discovery-based games, explanatory feedback led to better transfer than corrective feedback, with the same enjoyment. ([Moreno, 2004, via summary](https://serc.carleton.edu/resources/40398.html))
- For a pre-reader, "explain" means **show and say one short thing**:
  - Freeze the frame. Dim everything except the key players.
  - Draw a red line for the blocked pass, through the defender. Say: "Defender in the way!"
  - Rewind 1 second. Draw a green line to the open teammate. Say: "Pass to the open friend!"
  - **Replay the better choice** at normal speed so he sees it work.
- Use the **same short cue words** every time for one skill (for example "open friend," "space, go!," "stay goal-side"). Those words become the field cues (section 6).
- Keep the wrong-choice outcome **mild and funny**, not harsh. A bonk-and-wobble, not a sad trombone and red X. The Sesame Workshop guide recommends encouraging, step-by-step feedback on wrong answers. ([Sesame Workshop guide coverage](https://licensinginternational.org/news/sesame-workshop-shares-tips-for-creating-touch-screen-content-for-preschoolers))
- After a wrong choice, **let him try the same scenario again** right away. Getting it right the second time feels like a win and locks in the fix.

### Praise style

**Praise the process: what he looked at and chose.**

- Praising effort or process ("you worked hard") led children to persist and enjoy tasks more than praising ability ("you're smart"). ([Mueller & Dweck, 1998, summary](https://www.academicjobs.com/research-publication-news/praise-for-intelligence-harms-kids-motivation-dweck-study-or-academicjobs-11727))
- Parents' process praise at ages 1–3 predicted a growth mindset five years later. ([Gunderson et al., 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3655123))
- Good lines: "You looked up and found the open friend!" "You stayed between him and the goal!" Avoid: "You're a genius!" "You're the best player!"
- Big celebrations (goal animation, crowd) are fine as *outcome fun*. Keep the spoken praise about the choice.

## 3. Intro clips before a lesson

**Recommendation: use a short in-game animated demo (10–20 seconds) in the same view as the game. Treat real YouTube clips as an optional "watch with Dad/Mom" extra in parent mode.**

Why an in-game demo wins:

- **Modeling helps novices.** Children who watched an expert model learned new sport actions better than children given verbal or picture instructions. ([PMC10136588](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10136588)) Modeling is also one of the instructional supports that helps game-based learning. ([Wouters & van Oostendorp, 2013](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/13608021/05.pdf)) No studies test this with 5-year-olds and soccer decisions.
- **Same view, same characters, no translation.** A real broadcast clip uses a high side camera, 11v11, fast cuts, and commentary. A 5-year-old must map that onto your 2.5D 3v3 sprites. An in-game demo shows the exact pattern he will then be asked to choose.
- **You control the focus.** You can slow down, freeze, add the green "open" glow, and say the cue word. A real clip has many distracting details.
- **No legal, privacy, or network risk** (see below).

How to build the demo:

1. A favorite player sprite (for example a Barcelona kit) faces the decision.
2. Freeze. The narrator says the cue: "Defender coming? Find the open friend!"
3. The open teammate glows. The player passes. Goal or success.
4. Optional: show the *wrong* choice failing once, then the right one. Keep it under 20 seconds total.
5. Let him tap to skip after the first viewing. Re-show the demo only when a skill is new or after repeated misses.

About real clips:

- **Embedding YouTube on GitHub Pages is technically fine.** YouTube's embed feature is meant for this, and the YouTube API Terms apply to the embedded player. Use `youtube-nocookie.com` (privacy-enhanced mode). ([YouTube Help: Embed videos](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/171780))
- **Caveats:**
  - The page must send an HTTP Referer, or playback fails with error 153. ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/171780))
  - If the site is directed at children, YouTube's policies ask you to self-designate it as child-directed. Embedding videos labeled "made for kids" adds tracking limits. ([YouTube made-for-kids guide](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/guides/made_for_kids_status)) For a private family site, the practical risk is low, but it is a reason to keep embeds out of the child's main flow.
  - Owners can disable embedding or delete videos at any time. Clubs and leagues often geo-block or remove highlights. Your links will break.
  - The end screen shows "more videos" recommendations, which can pull a child off task.
- **Good sources if you do use clips:** official club channels (FC Barcelona, Real Madrid), official league channels, and coaching channels that show youth small-sided play. Pick 5–10-second moments by passing `start` and `end` parameters. Do not download and re-host clips: that copies copyrighted material.
- **Best use of real clips:** in parent mode, "Watch Messi find the open friend," with the parent pausing and asking the cue question. This adds the social element the research says matters most for young kids (section 6).

## 4. Spaced review and mastery

**Recommendation: Leitner-style boxes counted in sessions, not calendar days. Each session mixes the current skill with 1–3 review rounds of older skills. Mark a skill "mastered" after 3 first-try correct answers spread across at least 2 sessions.**

Evidence:

- **Preschoolers show the spacing effect.** Spaced practice beats massed practice for 3–5-year-olds in recall of pictures and words. ([Toppino, 1993](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03334130)) Expanding retrieval practice worked well for preschoolers learning names. ([Fritz et al., 2007, via Learning Scientists](https://learningscientists.org/blog/2022/2/3-1?format=amp)) Expanding and equal spacing ended up similar in 4–5-year-olds by the final test. ([Leonard et al., 2024](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11087082/)) So the exact schedule matters less than "come back to it later."
- **Interleaving helps telling situations apart.** A meta-analysis found a moderate-to-large interleaving effect, largest for category-type tasks. ([Brunmair & Richter, 2019, via PMC12470837](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12470837/)) 3-year-olds kept word meanings better with interleaved learning. ([same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12470837)) "Pass or dribble?" is a category task: he must see *which* situation he is in.
- **Caution:** interleaving feels harder. With a 5-year-old, mix only skills he has already met in drills. Do not interleave brand-new skills.

### Why sessions, not days

A 5-year-old plays when a parent allows it: maybe three times one week and none the next. Counting in **sessions played** keeps the schedule sensible. Add a small calendar rule: if more than 7 days pass, mark every non-new skill as due for a quick check.

### Concrete algorithm (fits localStorage)

Data per skill:

```json
{
  "skillId": "pass-to-open",
  "box": 0,
  "dueSession": 0,
  "firstTryCorrect": 0,
  "sessionsWithCorrect": [],
  "missStreak": 0,
  "unlocked": true
}
```

Plus one global object: `{ "sessionCount": 12, "lastPlayedAt": "2026-11-03" }`.

Box intervals, in sessions: `box 0 → 0` (this session), `1 → 1`, `2 → 2`, `3 → 4`, `4 → 8` (mastered; light upkeep).

Building a session (6–8 rounds):

1. **Current skill:** the lowest-box unlocked skill that is not mastered. Give it about 4–5 rounds.
2. **Review:** 1–3 skills where `dueSession <= sessionCount`, highest box first. One round each.
3. **Order:** start with an easy review round (a quick win). Put current-skill rounds in the middle, mixed with review. End with the easiest round of the session.
4. **Mini-match:** if 2+ skills are at box 2 or higher, add a mini-match that uses only those skills.

After each round:

- **First-try correct:** `firstTryCorrect += 1`, `missStreak = 0`. Add this session to `sessionsWithCorrect` if it is not there.
- **Wrong:** `missStreak += 1`. If `missStreak >= 2`, serve an easier variant or turn the hint glow back on.

At session end, per skill played:

- **Move up one box** if the skill was answered first-try correct in this session and had no misses.
- **Move down one box** (not to 0) if it had a miss.
- Set `dueSession = sessionCount + interval[box]`.
- **Mastered** when `box >= 3` *and* `firstTryCorrect >= 3` *and* `sessionsWithCorrect` covers 2 or more sessions. Mastery unlocks the next skill in the tree.

Keeping it fun:

- Never label review as "review." Review rounds use different kits, field spots, or a "remember this one?" star badge.
- Each scenario has 3–5 variants (mirror image, different spots, different kits) so he learns the pattern, not the screen.
- Wrap every `localStorage` read and write in try/catch. Safari can clear site data after long non-use, so add an export/import button in parent mode.

## 5. UI for pre-readers

**Recommendation: two or three huge tap targets, all instructions spoken, recorded audio rather than Web Speech, and rewards tied to playing and good choices rather than to perfect scores.**

### Tap targets and layout

- Children miss small targets often. Kids 7–11 missed touch targets 46% of the time, versus 32% for adults. ([Anthony et al., 2013](https://lisa-anthony.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/04/anthony-et-al-jpuc2013.pdf)) Preschoolers are less precise at every target size and worst with the smallest. ([Liew et al.](https://shdl.mmu.edu.my/11249/))
- Practical rules (not research-fixed numbers):
  - Make each choice at least **~80–100 CSS px** with a hit area larger than the visible button.
  - Offer **2–3 choices**, never more.
  - Let him **tap the player or the space itself** ("tap the friend you pass to") as well as icon buttons. Direct tapping is the most natural interaction.
  - Avoid double-tap, long-press, pinch, and drag. ([Sesame Workshop guide coverage](https://licensinginternational.org/news/sesame-workshop-shares-tips-for-creating-touch-screen-content-for-preschoolers))
  - Keep tappable things away from the bottom edge and corners. Kids rest their wrists there and trigger exits by accident. ([same source](https://licensinginternational.org/news/sesame-workshop-shares-tips-for-creating-touch-screen-content-for-preschoolers))
  - Design for landscape.
  - Give **instant feedback on every touch** (a sound plus a squash animation), even when the tap does nothing.

### Icons

- Use pictures of the action, not abstract symbols: a ball with an arrow to a teammate for "pass," a player with the ball and speed lines for "dribble," a shield between attacker and goal for "cover."
- Show the choice **on the field** where possible (glowing arrows to each option) instead of in a menu.

### Audio narration

- Every instruction must be spoken. Text can sit beside it for the parent.
- **Web Speech on iOS Safari is weak for this.**
  - `speechSynthesis.speak()` must start inside a tap handler, or it silently does nothing. ([WebKit bug 290497](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=290497), [Unity issue](https://issuetracker.unity3d.com/issues/webgl-web-speech-api-calls-do-not-return-audio-output-on-the-ios-safari-browser))
  - Only a fixed set of pre-installed voices is listed, and they sound robotic. Better voices the user downloads are not exposed to the page. ([WebKit bug 290497](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=290497))
  - Timing is unreliable, which matters for "freeze and speak" moments.
- **Better: pre-recorded audio files.** The game has a small, fixed set of lines (maybe 40–80). Options:
  - **Record a parent's voice.** This is the best option. It is warm, familiar, and it is the same voice he hears on the field (section 6).
  - Generate the files once with a good TTS tool and ship them as small `.mp3` or `.m4a` files.
- Unlock audio on the first tap (play a silent sound through Web Audio in the "Play" button handler). After that, scripted playback works.
- Keep narration short: 3–6 words per cue. Use no background music under speech; one study found it made toddlers' screen-to-real transfer worse. ([Barr review, NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2018-03/BarrConstraintsOppsMediaLearning.pdf))

### Rewards

- **Good loops:**
  - A **sticker book** that fills with a sticker for each session played and each skill mastered.
  - **Unlocking kits and favorite players** at skill milestones (master "pass to open" → unlock the Real Madrid away kit).
  - A **"my team" screen** where he picks kits. Choice and ownership motivate young kids.
- **Avoid:**
  - Random loot drops.
  - Streaks that punish missed days.
  - "Don't go yet!" rewards when he tries to stop.
  - Radesky's team found manipulative designs in 4 out of 5 apps used by young children, and noted children are especially susceptible to rewards flashed when they might otherwise quit. ([Michigan Medicine](https://www.michiganmedicine.org/health-lab/design-tricks-commonly-used-monetize-young-childrens-app-use))
- Rewards should never depend on a perfect score. Give the session sticker for finishing, and extra stickers for good choices.

### Avoiding frustration

- Use the 2-miss rule in section 4 to drop difficulty automatically.
- Never end a session on a failure. Insert one easy round before the end.
- Allow no "game over" and no losing progress.
- If he taps randomly (very fast taps, wrong 3+ times), pause and play the demo again, or suggest a break with a friendly character.

### Parent mode

Hide parent mode behind a gate a 5-year-old cannot pass. For example, "press and hold for 3 seconds" or a simple sum question shown as text.

Parent mode contains:

- **Progress per skill:** box, last seen, and mastered or not.
- **This week's cue words** and a matching field drill (section 6).
- **Settings:** session length cap, sound on/off, hint level, and turning on probabilistic outcomes in matches.
- **Optional real clips** to watch together (section 3).
- **Export/import progress** as JSON, since localStorage can be cleared.
- **Reset a skill** or unlock all for testing.

The Sesame Workshop guide also recommends parent controls to turn off distracting hotspots. ([source](https://licensinginternational.org/news/sesame-workshop-shares-tips-for-creating-touch-screen-content-for-preschoolers))

## 6. Transfer to the field

**Recommendation: do not expect the game alone to change how he plays. Treat the game as the place where he learns the cue words and patterns, and use a parent on the field to connect them. Same cue words, same pictures, same situations.**

Evidence on screen-to-field transfer:

- **Video training improves video tests, but field transfer is unproven.** In a review of football video training, 8 of 10 studies improved video-based decision scores. The authors note that retention and transfer tests are missing. ([Systematic review, 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9686440/)) A scoping review found lab-based decision training "limited and inconsistent" with unclear on-field transfer, while game-based field training worked better. ([Scoping review](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4097849))
- **A youth pilot shows transfer is possible with physical practice.** Young volleyball players improved on both video and on-field decisions after video training, and the authors suggest pairing it with physical practice. ([LJMU pilot, 2025](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/27618/))
- **Video game play alone shows weak effects.** Teen players who played video games had slightly higher tactical knowledge, but the difference was not significant. ([Brazil study, 2021](https://doaj.org/article/20bc9701f6fd4b7aaa610765c8bbc83e))
- **All of this is teens and adults.** Nothing tests 5-year-olds. Young children have a known "transfer deficit" from screens to the real world. It is strongest under age 3, but children still benefit greatly from support. ([Barr, NICHD](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/sites/default/files/2018-03/BarrConstraintsOppsMediaLearning.pdf); [Frontiers 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02195/full))
- **What reduces the transfer gap:** repetition, interactivity, and **an adult who connects the screen to real life**. Video chat alone was not enough; co-viewers who scaffold learning helped. ([PMC10783145](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10783145); [ZERO TO THREE](https://www.zerotothree.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Screen-Sense_-Executive-Summary.pdf))
- **Socially interactive** is one of the four pillars of learning from apps. Children learn best with parents and peers. ([Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/educational-apps.html))

Design choices that help transfer:

1. **Shared cue words.** Each skill has one cue of 1–3 words, used in the game narration, the parent screen, and on the field. Examples: "Open friend!", "Look up!", "Space, go!", "Goal-side!", "Squeeze!"
2. **"Look up" as a core skill.** Elite players who scan before receiving the ball pass better. ([Jordet et al., 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.553813/pdf)) The game can teach this directly: before choosing, he taps a "look" button, or the camera does a quick look-around, and the open teammate is revealed.
3. **Perspective.** A third-person, behind-the-player view is the right default. It is FIFA-like and shows the needed info. The player's-eye view seems to aid realism and confidence in adult studies, but no study tests it for transfer. ([Review, 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9686440/); [refereeing perspective study](https://fis.dshs-koeln.de/de/publications/the-role-of-perspective-in-refereeing-insights-from-a-video-based/)) A cheap middle ground: keep the camera low and behind *his* player, at about kid height, not a high broadcast view.
4. **Match his real game.** Use 3v3, a small field, no goalkeepers (or match his league rules), and kids-size goals. The game situations should look like what he sees on Saturday.
5. **Pair each skill with a backyard drill.** Parent mode shows a 2-minute drill per skill. Example for "pass to open": parent plus a cone "defender"; he must pass to the side where the cone is not.
6. **Ask, don't tell, on the field.** Use the cue as a question: "Where's the open friend?" This matches guided questioning in youth coaching. ([Iowa Soccer Play-Practice-Play](https://www.iowasoccer.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/169/2023/09/coach_play_practice_play.pdf))
7. **After a real game,** open the matching drill in the app: "Remember when you passed to Sam? Let's find the open friend here too."

## 7. Existing apps and what to learn

**Finding: no app found targets 5–6-year-olds for soccer decisions. Existing tools target older players, use text, and have no independent evidence with children.**

| App | Who it is for | What to borrow | What to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Soccer IntelliGym](https://soccer.intelligym.com/) ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6538727452)) | Older youth and adults; cognitive training | Short, repeated decision reps; adaptive difficulty | Abstract tasks that do not look like soccer; claims are vendor-only |
| [Pivot Soccer](https://mwm.ai/apps/pivot-soccer/6760321143) | Youth players and families | Named habits ("scan," "habits") and reports for parents | Large skill count (82 habits) is far too much for age 5 |
| [A-Champs ROX](https://a-champs.com/blogs/magazine/improve-soccer-decision-making) | Physical reaction-light training | Links screen-style cues to physical movement | Needs hardware; trains reaction more than choice |
| [MatchdayIQ](https://apps.apple.com/app/id6763950043), [Soccer Training IQ](https://mwm.ai/ko/apps/soccer-training-iq/6737512667) | Teens and adults | Pause-and-pick scenario format | Text-heavy quizzes, subscriptions |
| FIFA / EA FC (his favorite) | Everyone | Kits, favorite players, celebrations, the "my team" feeling | Fast pace, many buttons, no explanation of why a choice worked |

Lessons:

- **The "freeze, choose, see outcome" format is common and well-liked** in adult tactical trainers. Your game's core loop is the right one.
- **The gap is age-appropriate design:** spoken cues, 2–3 choices, a replay of the better option, and a parent bridge to the field. Nothing found does this.
- **Treat vendor claims with caution.** No app had independent studies with young children. ([search summary of TrustRadius listing](https://trustradius.com/products/soccer-intelligym/reviews))

## Where the evidence is thin

- **No study** tests screen-based soccer decision training in children under about 8.
- **Session length** for 5-year-olds is based on guidelines and indirect studies, not direct tests.
- **Errorless vs. error-based learning** in typical 5-year-olds is mixed. The recommended middle path (hints first, then real choices) is a judgment call.
- **First-person vs. third-person view** for transfer has no direct test.
- Several sources above are summaries of papers, not the full papers. Check the originals before quoting exact numbers.
